01 / 05the problem

Wireworld’s rule fits in one line: a conductor fires when 1 or 2 of its neighbours are heads.

Climbing from that rule to a processor that runs programs takes signals, gates, arithmetic and memory, with every rung verified. The climb is the last of eight acts in one canvas engine, from Langton’s ant to Wireworld. The engine keeps shared typed-array grids, and each act supplies only its own step and seed functions.

02 / 05how it works

Wireworld has 4 states: empty, head, tail and conductor.

A head moves one cell per tick along a conductor, and the tail behind it keeps the wire one-way.

Two cells are built in that raw rule. The NAND cell is a 5-cell junction fed by a clock loop. When a and b both arrive with the clock pulse, 3 heads meet and the junction stays dark. The memory loop (the repo’s loop register) holds a bit as an electron circling a loop, written through a diode, read by a tap and cleared by a timed pulse.

The CPU is SAP-1, the textbook Simple-As-Possible teaching CPU, extended with stores and branches: 8-bit data, 4-bit addresses and 16 words of memory. It is one flat netlist of the two primitives. Each clock, every NAND settles in topological order, then every flip-flop samples its input and all of them commit at once.

An instruction takes up to six clocks: three to fetch it, up to three to run it. On each clock, either one register drives the bus and one latches it, or nothing moves on the bus. The hero replays the netlist’s own record of the Fibonacci program: 688 clocks from the first fetch to the halt.

03 / 05the asynchronous wall

Hand-wired Wireworld gates failed when composed.

A long lead on one input corrupted the other, and the wires between gates carried signals backward until they oscillated.

The fix is the one silicon uses: latch every signal in a flip-flop once per clock and let the NAND logic settle in between.

NAND is functionally complete and the flip-flop holds state, so two primitives, a NAND gate and a D flip-flop, cover the whole machine.

The memory loop needs the same timing. A clear pulse that meets the circulating electron leaves 0 electrons in the grid. The same pulse 2 ticks late leaves the bit readable.

04 / 05results

Five test programs run against expected output: 5 + 3 and 9 − 4, a countdown, a running sum, and Fibonacci until 8-bit overflow trips the carry flag.

SAP-2 is a bigger CPU on the same two primitives, with a stack, and runs multiply, divide, GCD, primes and bubble sort. A separate build in Conway’s Life, a level below Wireworld, makes gates from glider collisions. Its routed half-adder runs in the raw rule; run once per bit, with the sum and carries passed between runs, it adds 511+1=512. The CPU does not run on it.

Proven: the NAND cell and the memory loop in the raw rule; a full adder, a 4-bit counter and a D flip-flop compiled to it, matching their netlists on every clock; the CPU and its five programs as a netlist.

Not proven: the whole CPU in the raw rule. Every gate’s inputs must arrive on the same beat, which takes planned delay lines, and the router manages that for only about a dozen gates so far.
