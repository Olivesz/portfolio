01 · the situation

One history, no answer key

Markets switch moods. Calm rallies, choppy stretches and selloffs come and go, and none of them is announced.

A model that labels those moods has nothing to check its labels against. Its answer for any given day can be wrong and still look right on a chart. Most backtests are a single number computed once on one history, with no ground truth to check that number against.

The platform runs fully offline on markets like this one, built so every model has real structure to find, and the answer to each one is known.

02 · the idea

Plant the answer, then grade

Build worlds where the answer is planted. An estimator has to find the plant, and stay quiet in a world with nothing planted, before it is allowed near a real price.

The two halves have names. Size: on a world where the effect is absent, the test does not fire. Power: on a world where it is present by construction, the test detects it. A test that never fires has perfect size and is worth nothing, so both worlds are needed.

No estimator is trusted until it passes a gate on data where the answer is already known.

03 · the build

From a planted world to a sized book

The steps below build the regime model and the gates around it, then the book that model used to drive, then the overlay that sizes the book. That regime-gated book lost to plain equal weight under a pre-registered test, so the shipped book is now equal weight, and the regime-gated one runs beside it as the control every new construction must beat. First, the whole machine on one page.

step 01 · the planted world

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

A hidden chain, two numbers a day

The regime model’s gate builds its world from three hidden moods. Each day a Markov chain either stays in the current mood or moves, and the mood emits two numbers: the day’s return, and the log of its volatility. The gate fixes the synthetic ground truth: the length, the switching odds, and each mood’s centre and spread.

On real data the same two features come from the market: the daily market return and the log of its realized volatility, annualized, over a 10-day window.

step 02 · fitting it

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

EM finds the plant

The model is a hidden Markov model: three states, each with its own centre and spread in the two features, and a table of day-to-day switching odds. It is fitted by expectation-maximisation, which alternates two moves.

First, score every day against the current guesses with a forward sweep and a backward sweep through time. Then move each guess to the weighted average of the days that belong to it. Repeat, and break off once the fit stops improving. The fit starts 3 times, the later starts nudged at random, and keeps the best.

On real data the same gate asks history to agree: more than 0.7 of March 2020 labelled Bear or Volatile, and more than 0.7 of 2017 labelled Bull.

step 03 · no future

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

Read it without the future

A fitted model can answer two questions about any day. Filtered: given the data up to that day, which state is it in now? Smoothed: given the whole sample, which state was it in? The smoothed answer is cleaner because it uses days that had not happened yet.

The regime gate’s world is so clean that both readings agree on almost every day. The next two figures use a murkier market from the opening figure’s generator, fitted the same way and read through the same two features as real data.

Every trading decision reads filtered probabilities only. The model is refit walk-forward: first after 750 days, then every 21 trading days.

The gate itself cuts the last 300 days. The trading system gets the same test: drop the last 200 days, and every earlier target weight has to stay within a billionth.

step 04 · the label

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

From probabilities to a label

A filtered probability jitters from day to day. Trading on its raw leader would turn the book over on noise, so the label follows two rules.

The label flips only after a confirmation streak, and holds for a minimum dwell unless the new state is near-certain. A new state has to lead above 0.65 for 5 straight days, and the label stays put for at least 10 days after a change, unless the new state is above 0.90 on the day it is confirmed.

step 05 · fat tails

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

Fat tails fake regimes

Real returns have fat tails: big days come far more often than a bell curve allows. A Gaussian model tends to explain them by inventing extra states. On simulated fat-tailed worlds with 3 true states, Gaussian model selection picked too many states for 7 of 8 seeds (6 picked five), against 4 of 8 on plain Gaussian worlds; the Student-t picked too many for 3 of 8.

The shipped engine is a Student-t HMM instead. Each state emits a Student-t, and each day gets a weight that shrinks as it lands further from its state’s centre, so one outlier cannot drag the fit. It runs with the same features, refits, label rules and causality test as the Gaussian engine.

step 06 · the book

the control · prices → regimes → signals → HRP + BL → overlay

The control: from a label to a book

This was the shipped book until 13 October, and it still runs as the control. Every 21 trading days the label picks a signal mix, the mix ranks the names, and the book is a hierarchical-risk-parity backbone with a Black-Litterman tilt, which feeds the signal into the weights as a view (the BL in HRP + BL). Equal weight replaced all of that in the shipped book; the overlay in the next step sizes either one.

step 07 · the overlay

either book · equal weight or HRP + BL → overlay → t+1

Lever times the tighter brake

The size of the whole book is set last. A lever aims the book’s volatility at its target by dividing the target by a forecast of the book’s own volatility, capped at 1.5×. Two brakes can only hold it back: a tail-loss brake and a drawdown throttle. Exposure is the lever times whichever brake is tighter.

The forecast is a small regression, refit every 21 days: the book’s squared daily returns, averaged over the last 1, 5 and 22 days, predict the next day’s. A short window reacts to a shock, a long one remembers the regime, and the regression learns how much to trust each. That is the forecast box in the machine above.

The exposure is smoothed over 5 days, and a ±15% tilt on the sign of the market’s recent momentum rides inside the cap. Gate X27 pins the overlay’s direction and reach: the drawdown throttle, the lever’s cap and the financing charge; the tilt has its own gate.

Beside the book runs a small pairs sleeve: pairs of names that move together, with a Kalman-filtered hedge ratio, traded when their spread stretches, at 10% gross. Its returns are added to the book’s each day.

04 · what broke

The brake that pushed the wrong way

Every gate passed, and the book still fell short of its own plan.

Realized volatility sat at 6.9% against a 13% target, so the question was where the return went. The gates themselves were the first place to look.

The drawdown throttle shrinks the book as it falls from its last high: full size down to a −8% drawdown, then a straight line to 0.25 at −20%. As written, the line’s span came out negative, so it ran the wrong way.

The repair was pre-registered: the findings, the two repairs, the expected outcomes and the kill criteria were written down before the repaired system ran. It shipped with the missing gate, and the old numbers stay in the repo as the baseline row.

Two kill lines were written before the run: if Sharpe (return per unit of risk) fell by more than 0.05, the leverage would not ship; if financing ate half the leverage gain, the leverage would be dropped.

A later gate checks the exposure cap under an extreme tilt instead of assuming it, and its record says why: an inverted overlay hid in this codebase once. Direction is now something the suite tests.

A brake that braked at the top and let go in a crash passed every gate, because no gate asked which way it pushed.

05 · results

Against equal weight and SPY

The shipped book holds about equal weight’s Sharpe at 64% of its drawdown, and beats SPY’s Sharpe at 56% of its drawdown.

That is risk control, not alpha: equal weight and SPY still win on raw return, and on Sharpe over cash the book lands just short of equal weight, which the repo reports as a failure.

Since 2026-08-17 a CI job has logged the unlevered KRONOS-TRADE variant, not the shipped book, before each session: after 35 trading days it is down 2.38%, against SPY’s +0.70% and equal weight’s −2.25%, a gap of about one standard error, too soon to confirm or refute the backtest. Frozen with every US setting and rerun on Japan, Europe and Asia-EM, the shipped book kept roughly half the local index’s drawdown, but equal weight on the same names beat its Sharpe in all three markets, and the index did in Japan and Europe.

Beside the book sit 35 pre-registered experiments, with negative results reported as plainly as positive ones: stat-arb is dead, random-matrix denoising does not help here, duration models do not beat the plain HMM, and the trading system does not beat the S&P on CAGR.
